Retirement

Craig Manning retires after 36 years at UCLA

Craig Manning leaves UCLA after a career that reshaped how we understand fluids, melts, and chemical change in the deep Earth.

Jonathan M. Aurnou3 min read

Craig Manning retired this summer after 36 years at UCLA, leaving a remarkable legacy in experimental and metamorphic petrology, geochemistry, and the broader life of EPSS.

Craig is a world leader in understanding what fluids actually do in the deep Earth, particularly in subduction zones. It seems almost obvious now that slabs carry water and other volatiles downward and then release a chemically potent briny soup that profoundly alters the overlying mantle and helps drive the processes we see in volcanic arcs. But Craig played a central role in establishing the experimental and thermodynamic basis for that picture. Among his most important results was showing that, at sufficiently high pressures and temperatures, silicate solubilities in aqueous fluids become enormous, ultimately blurring the distinction between hydrous melts and dilute aqueous solutions.

Craig has always taken no prisoners in the laboratory. His experiments are elegant, deceptively simple, and exceptionally rigorous—a combination recognized by AGU with the 2017 Norman L. Bowen Award. He brought that same petrologic perspective into the field, from Greenland, where his early work first drew him toward fluid-rock interactions, to Tibet, where he helped identify distinctive terranes and contributed to UCLA’s emergence as a major center for studies of the India-Asia collision.

His contributions to UCLA have been just as substantial. Craig served as EPSS Chair from 2008–2012, during an unusually consequential period of faculty growth. He was an amazing chair, somehow knowing every detail of the department for four years—a nearly impossible feat. He went on to broader leadership at UCLA as an Associate Dean, a position he continues to hold, and internationally through the Deep Carbon Observatory.

There is also a characteristic Craig Manning way of doing all of this. In meetings he listens carefully, generally longer than most, and when he finally speaks, he packs a huge punch. Thus, the same economy and precision that characterize his experiments are also evident in the way he approaches scientific and institutional questions.

Fittingly, just weeks before his retirement, Craig was elected to the National Academy of Sciences—a wonderful recognition of a career that has changed how we think about fluids in the deep Earth.
