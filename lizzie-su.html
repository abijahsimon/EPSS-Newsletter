Lizzie Su on life in EPSS

Through mentorship, fieldwork, and a close-knit EPSS community, undergraduate Lizzie Su found a path in geophysics — and is helping other students find their own.

By Benjamin Paul

UCLA Division of Physical Sciences · 6 min read

Lizzie Su had already learned how to program a data-acquisition system when one of her UCLA professors suggested they take the next step: hike up Mount Whitney and put it to the test.

The excursion, part of an advanced coding course taught by Professor Jon Aurnou, brought together two parts of Su’s education that have come to define her experience in EPSS: building technical skills in the classroom and finding opportunities to use them in the world outside it.

It was not the path she expected when she arrived at UCLA.

Su entered as an environmental science major, interested in eventually working at the intersection of science and policy. During her first year, however, she realized she missed the math and physics she had enjoyed in high school.

Then she began taking classes in EPSS.

“I realized, ‘Hey, I like this a lot,’” Su said.

Earth science was not entirely new to her. Growing up in Mountain House, California, Su had encountered it through activities including Science Olympiad. But she had not realized it was something she could pursue as a college major.

An introductory course with Kevin Coffey, one of the first professors many students encounter in EPSS, helped change that. Su was drawn to the department’s small classes, field trips, and hands-on approach. Geophysics offered a chance to return to math and physics while continuing to explore environmental questions that mattered to her.

It also broadened those questions. Where her environmental science studies had focused largely on climate change, EPSS introduced her to earthquakes, landslides, and other natural hazards.

Along the way, Su found something else she had not necessarily been looking for: a community.

There’s been so much support from basically everyone I’ve ever interacted with — the other students, the graduate students, faculty. I’m so happy to be a geophysics major now.

Lizzie Su

Making EPSS more visible

Su became involved with the EPSS Family Mentorship Program after seeing advertisements for its weekly tea time, where students gather informally over food. She later joined the program’s board and helped organize social events and other activities that bring undergraduates and graduate students together.

The program places students into mentorship “families” with peers at different stages of their education. It has also introduced “no-jargon talks,” short presentations in which graduate students explain their research to undergraduates without the specialized language that can make a new field feel inaccessible.

For Su, making those possibilities visible is important.

As a child, she had relatively little exposure to careers in the physical sciences. Both of her parents work in medicine. Looking back, she said simply seeing people who had built lives in other scientific fields, and who enjoyed what they were doing, would have made an impression.

Now she has become one of those people for younger students.

At UCLA’s Exploring Your Universe science festival, Su has helped introduce hundreds of children to geology through “stratigraphy in a jar,” an EPSS activity in which children layer brightly coloured sand in glass vials while learning how sediment accumulates over time. The booth was so popular that Su and her fellow volunteers ran out of jars well before the event ended.

Research and what comes next

Su’s own opportunities have continued to grow. As an undergraduate researcher in Professor Lingsen Meng’s group, she has studied earthquake rupture processes and is now working on a project using computer simulations and machine learning to explore tsunami early warning. She has also worked on near-surface hazards including landslides.

But for Su, the value of those experiences extends beyond any single research subject. Her EPSS coursework has given her experience in coding, data analysis, field methods, and instrumentation, skills she believes will allow her to move among different scientific problems as her interests develop.

That matters as she prepares for her next step. Su plans to apply to Ph.D. programs this fall and is considering continuing in seismology, while remaining open to other directions.

Her advice to other undergraduates comes directly from taking a direction she had not anticipated herself.

“I was a little bit uncertain about whether or not it was something I should pursue further,” Su said of geoscience. “Hopefully, other students will be encouraged to take the chance.”

She also encourages students to seek out the communities around their interests. Faculty helped Su discover EPSS. Graduate students and fellow undergraduates helped her find a place within it. Those relationships led to research, field experiences, mentorship, and opportunities she could not have anticipated when she arrived at UCLA.

“There’s so much vibrant research going on in the department,” Su said. “It’s important to get their work out there for people to know.”

Su came to UCLA unsure exactly where her environmental interests would take her. Now, she has a much wider set of possibilities and is helping make sure the students coming behind her know those possibilities are there too.

Benjamin Paul
