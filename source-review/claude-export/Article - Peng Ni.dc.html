Exploring planetary history with Peng Ni

The UCLA planetary scientist combines laboratory experiments, isotope measurements, and fieldwork to study Earth, the Moon, and beyond.

By Benjamin Paul

UCLA Division of Physical Sciences · 7 min read

The first time Peng Ni had an opportunity to work with samples brought back from the Moon by the Apollo missions, he was not planning on making lunar science a major part of his career.

The samples changed that. Ni had encountered something unlike the terrestrial materials he was accustomed to studying. The opportunity came somewhat unexpectedly during graduate school, but working with material from another world became a special moment in his early career, and the beginning of a research interest that has followed him ever since.

Today, much of Ni’s research moves between the Moon, meteorites, and diamonds formed far beneath Earth’s surface. The objects can be remarkably small. The questions are not. How did the Moon form and evolve? How did asteroids develop their distinct cores and mantles? What can material preserved deep inside Earth tell us about processes that took place billions of years ago?

Ni approaches those questions in part by recreating conditions that scientists cannot directly observe. His laboratory at UCLA uses high-pressure, high-temperature experiments to simulate environments inside Earth and other rocky bodies. Its furnaces and piston-cylinder apparatus can reach temperatures as high as 1,700 degrees Celsius and pressures found deep inside planets, allowing Ni and his students to essentially manufacture planetary materials in the laboratory.

The Sapphire Lab gives them another way to investigate the past. Its centerpiece is the Nu Sapphire, a specialized mass spectrometer installed at UCLA in 2023. The instrument can detect minute differences in the isotopic makeup of elements including potassium, iron, copper, and magnesium, chemical fingerprints that can reveal where a material came from and what happened to it. When UCLA’s instrument arrived, Ni said, it was only the 20th Sapphire produced worldwide.

Ni has compared this kind of work to another interest that began long before his scientific career: detective fiction. An avid reader since high school, he credits it with helping shape the logical way he approaches problems.

He approaches geochemistry much like a detective approaches a case: identify the mystery, consider possible explanations, and follow the evidence. The satisfaction comes when the clues finally add up and a difficult scientific problem begins to make sense.

Lunar glass and diamond time capsules

The Apollo samples have provided some particularly intriguing evidence.

Ni’s earlier work helped change scientists’ understanding of the Moon’s interior. Once thought to have lost most of its volatile elements during its formation, the Moon appears to have held onto more of them than expected, raising new questions about its earliest history.

His group is now looking at tiny green and orange glass beads created by volcanic eruptions on the Moon billions of years ago. Working with postdoc Susana Hoyos and collaborators, the team recreated lunar glass in the laboratory and used it to reexamine samples returned by Apollo 15 and Apollo 17. Their results challenge previous interpretations of how sulfur escaped from magma during ancient lunar eruptions.

Some of Ni’s smallest samples carry evidence from much closer to home, and deep underground. In another recent study, Ni and collaborators examined microscopic pockets of fluid trapped inside diamonds from Sierra Leone.

Diamonds can act as tiny geological time capsules. They preserve material from deep within Earth that would otherwise be altered or destroyed.

Peng Ni

Using UCLA’s Sapphire, the researchers found evidence that the fluids inside these diamonds had an extraordinary history. Their chemical signature points to material from ancient oceanic crust that was carried deep into Earth and eventually contributed to the fluids from which the diamonds grew.

Material that once existed near Earth’s surface can disappear into the planet and leave a record of its journey inside a diamond hundreds of millions or even billions of years later.

Fieldwork and mentoring

Igneous petrology in the field

Video: Peng Ni, UCLA. Watch on YouTube

Ni is especially passionate about educating the next generation of geoscientists.

“One of the things that makes this department special is how dedicated it is to bringing students into the field. There is no better classroom than the natural world and I hope we can continue to grow in this area.”

Peng Ni

In October, he and EPSS Professor Amy Mainzer led a 17-person group of graduate students, faculty, and volunteers onto dry lakebeds near Pahrump, Nevada, in search of meteorites. The expedition was part of a course on the origin and evolution of the solar system that combines Mainzer’s background in astronomy with Ni’s in geology. Students found about three meteorites apiece.

“Thanks to California’s unique geology, UCLA is perfectly positioned to offer a strong, thriving field-based teaching and research program,” Ni said. “I have colleagues at East Coast universities who fly their students here for that very reason.”

Hugo Abe, Ni’s first graduate mentee, found meteorites, watched the Orionid meteor shower, and celebrated his birthday during the expedition. The group surprised him with a cake on a dry lakebed in the Mojave. Experiences like these provide context that simply cannot be gained from textbooks or learned in the laboratory.

Ni’s own path into planetary science took time, something he has encouraged younger scientists to embrace. Nearly four years of his Ph.D. passed before he felt he had found the scientific questions that truly interested him. His advice to graduate students is not to limit themselves to projects handed to them by their advisors, but to develop questions of their own.

“Finding our true passion in the area is critical for the scientific career in the long run,” he said.

Outside the lab, Ni has pursued photography for nearly a decade, particularly landscapes and the places he encounters while traveling. He is drawn to the way photographers can capture their own view of a landscape. He plans to begin sharing some of his work on his lab website.

Back at UCLA, there is no shortage of questions competing for his attention. His research now stretches from the early history of the Moon to the movement of material deep inside Earth, while his students are beginning to pursue questions of their own. That breadth is something Ni encourages. The advice he gives young scientists is simple: explore widely, find the problems that genuinely interest you, and give yourself the freedom to follow them.

Benjamin Paul
