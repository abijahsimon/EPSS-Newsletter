Understanding Earth’s deforming continents

After more than thirty years at UCLA, Gilles Peltzer retires having reshaped how we understand the way continents bend, break, and slide.

By Paul Mistral

Photographs from the EPSS-262 field archive

2026 Issue 6 min read

Professor Peltzer has dedicated his career to uncovering how Earth’s continents move and change shape. Scientists have long understood the basics of plate tectonics, but the deformation of continents — especially in complex, long-evolving regions like Asia — has remained a puzzle.

For over thirty years Peltzer has been at the forefront of solving it, employing everything from hands-on experiments and field observations to cutting-edge satellite technology.

A block of Plasticine, and a new model of Asia

His journey began in the 1980s, when he set out to explore the geometry of subduction zones — areas where one tectonic plate slides beneath another. By analysing the forces acting on these slabs, he demonstrated that the arcuate shape of subduction zones could evolve into a simple helical form on the sphere.

His most famous contribution came during his PhD. Using a simple experiment with Plasticine, he and his advisor showed how the collision of India with Asia caused large blocks of Earth’s crust to be squeezed out sideways between major transform faults. Though debated, this Extrusion Tectonics model revolutionised scientific understanding of continental deformation and inspired generations of researchers.

A simple experiment with modelling clay changed how a generation of scientists pictured the collision of India with Asia.

Watching the ground move from space

Peltzer was among the first to use high-resolution satellite images to track fault movements in Asia, measuring how landforms such as river channels and glacial moraines had been displaced by earthquakes over millennia. His work with radar interferometry (InSAR) proved that the technique could sense the subtle, often invisible ways the crust shifts between, during, and after earthquakes.

In collaboration with CNES researchers, he produced the first-ever image of an earthquake’s static displacement field from space — the 1992 Landers earthquake in California. He also discovered how crustal fluids, trapped in and around fault zones, play a key role in stress relaxation in the shallow crust after an earthquake, influencing aftershocks and even triggering new quakes on nearby faults.

His research has also revealed surprising behaviours in Earth’s crust. Observations of asymmetric displacement across a surface rupture produced by a large earthquake in Tibet showed that the crust can act like a non-linear elastic material — its stiffness changing depending on whether it is being stretched or squeezed.

Tibet, and an unexpected signal

With colleagues at JPL, Peltzer investigated the present-day displacement field of the Tibetan Plateau using the archive of InSAR data from ESA’s Sentinel-1 mission and the recently launched NASA-ISRO NISAR mission. Early results highlight the concentration of strain along major faults, confirming the primary role of transform faults in accommodating the shortening between India and Asia — as proposed in the early Extrusion Tectonics model, a theory still debated today.

An unexpected finding from this research is the vertical velocity field of the Tibetan Plateau, which reveals broad-scale subsidence linked to the degradation of permafrost in a warming climate.

Campfires in the Mojave

Beyond research, Peltzer is a dedicated teacher. He developed and taught courses on tectonics, earthquakes, remote sensing, and radar interferometry, always emphasising hands-on learning. In the geologic playground of Southern California’s deserts, his classes often included field trips where students could see firsthand how the landscape tells the story of Earth’s restless surface.

His most cherished memories are the endless conversations with students around a campfire after a long day of studying earthquake faults, volcanoes, sand dunes, and the many wonders of geology in the hot California desert.
