Art & Science · Q&A

Two scientists who paint

One has painted since childhood and never stopped. The other picked up a brush twenty years ago on a whim. Both find the same thing in it that they find in research.

In their own words — written answers from David Jewitt and Hilke Schlichting

9 min read

David Jewitt

Professor, Earth, Planetary, and Space Sciences

How did you start painting?

I have no background and no real knowledge of art. I just started painting one day about 20 years ago when I thought “why not give it a try?” and I liked the results. On the one hand, being a total novice is an advantage, because I’m not bogged down by any rules or preconceptions. I don’t know what the rules are. I’ve heard of “color theory” but I don’t know what it is. On the other hand, that makes me an art ignoramus.

Classical paintings, like classical music, hold little interest for me because they are so predictable — another king, another bowl of fruit, another Jesus, another farm scene. I like the wild stuff a lot more. By trial and error, I discovered that my main interest is in the materiality of the paint, in texture and color and abstraction. I just want to make something that interests me even though I can’t specify why.

When I have made a painting that I like I can hardly believe that I did it. But I don’t take myself too seriously; art is subjective and I’m not trying to do anything profound. I just paint for fun.

What do you work with?

I use a wide range of materials, from house paint, the thick glue used in sticking bathroom tiles to the wall, seeds, cement, shells, sticks, string, bits of metal, stones, sand, epoxy, plastic foam, even meat skewers, to you name it. The different materials are part of the fun. Mostly I use acrylic paints — acrylic is basically made from plastic micro-flakes in solution — because they’re easy to clean, but I’ve used oil paints a few times.

What’s the connection between painting and science?

I don’t think there is a direct connection, but I do see a weird indirect similarity between the way I paint and the way I do science. I usually start a painting with, at best, a raw idea of what I want. I have a simple idea and I feel a burst of energy and enthusiasm. Then I begin. As time goes on and the painting starts to look hopeless, I kick into high gear and then I struggle to figure a way to salvage my lost effort. Sometimes that fails and I paint over it and start again. Sometimes the finished painting bears no relation to the one I had planned.

It usually ends when I’m so sick of a painting that I can’t bear to look at it any more. Weeks and months later, though, I can come back and, if I like what I’ve done, I wonder if it happened by accident and ask myself “how did I do that?”.

My way of doing science is like that, too. I’ll start a project based on a simple idea and a wave of enthusiasm that sweeps through my mind, without having a clear idea about the details of the project or a proper understanding of the background. Then I hit a brick wall, and I have to think more carefully. This chaotic, start-stop style is slow and inefficient, but by not planning too carefully I do gain the advantage that I am relatively open to making discoveries and doing new things.

It’s easier to see or do something new when you have no preconceptions, or have only a faint — and maybe incorrect — idea about what is already known. In fact, many of the best things I’ve done in science come from working impulsively like this. I feel like I stumble into my paintings and my research projects, but also that working in this way is what keeps me fresh.

Hilke Schlichting

Professor, Earth, Planetary, and Space Sciences

How did you start painting?

I have been painting for as long as I can remember. Or perhaps, more precisely, I have never stopped. I feel like all children draw and paint, but then, someday, as they grow older, they stop. I see similarities to science here, as I think children, in general, are curious about the world around them and ask all the fundamental questions. But as they grow older, many of them stop. And just like with painting, I feel I never stopped asking these basic, fundamental questions about the world and universe around us. In my research, I deal with questions related to understanding where we come from. So one might say I just “never really grew up.”

What’s the connection between painting and science?

I think I am an inherently creative person, and art and science are both highly creative activities, except that in my art creativity is expressed visually, whereas in science it is more conceptual and mathematical, often in the form of equations. Both, to me, can be incredibly beautiful. If you go back a few hundred years, you will find many artists who were also great scientists, and vice versa — think of Leonardo da Vinci, for example. I think it is only in relatively recent times that these two disciplines have diverged so much.

What do you paint, and with what?

The subject matter of my art is not directly influenced by my research on exoplanets and planet formation. Instead, my art is mostly inspired by travels and experiences I have had. I paint to process these experiences and emotions and to capture some of these fleeting moments so that I can share them with others. Through my paintings, I hope to make the viewer curious about the world around them. My paintings are mostly representational and often depict places I have visited, including historic sites, many of them in Asia — a continent that continues to fascinate me. I am really drawn to color and light. I mostly paint with acrylics on canvas, because they are easy to use and dry quickly, but I also experiment with pastels and gouache from time to time.

Although my research is not the subject matter of my paintings, I do see many similarities between my scientific and artistic practice. For example, both start with an idea or vision that exists only in my head and is gradually transformed into something real and tangible. I find this creative process incredibly satisfying. Both painting and research also involve a great deal of problem-solving; only the tools are different. Finally, both require a certain degree of instinct and intuition, as well as deep focus and attention, which I find exhilarating.

I paint for the same reason that I do research: I simply enjoy doing it.
