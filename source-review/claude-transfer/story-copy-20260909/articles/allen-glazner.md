When Allen Glazner returned to UCLA in February to deliver the 2026 Earth, Planetary, and Space Sciences Distinguished Alumni Lecture, he began with a statement that could double as a summary of his scientific career.

"I like science best when what we think we know turns out to be wrong," he told the audience.

Glazner, who earned his Ph.D. in geology from UCLA in 1981, has spent decades studying magmatism, granites, and the processes that build continental crust. His lecture, "The Mythology of Magma Chambers," focused on his argument that one of geology's most familiar images, the large reservoir of molten rock sitting beneath a volcano, is often deeply misleading. But the talk also revealed something broader about how Glazner approaches science: he is drawn to the moment when evidence stops fitting the prevailing explanation.

Glazner grew up in Upland, California, near the San Gabriel Mountains, and studied geology at Pomona College just as plate tectonics was transforming the field. As an undergraduate, he began investigating volcanic rocks in the Mojave Desert and their possible relationship to subduction. When he arrived at UCLA in 1976, he continued pursuing questions about volcanism and tectonics, even though, as he said in his lecture, "UCLA didn't have faculty doing what I wanted to do. I just did my own thing."

What UCLA did have, he said, was an unusually strong community of faculty and graduate students, along with a culture of field-based learning that helped shape him. He recalled student-led trips through Owens Valley, Mono Lake, and the Mojave Desert, and credited professors including Art Montana, Gary Ernst, Jerry Schubert, and Peter Bird, among others, with influencing his development as a scientist.

"The faculty were so good and my cohort of students was so good that it was the best place I could have gone," he said.

Decades later, while testing whether shallow magma chambers were thermally viable, Glazner found himself drawing on what he had learned in a UCLA graduate course taught by geophysicist Jerry Schubert. The class, he said, gave him both the knowledge and the confidence to make the calculations.

"I would not have done that without Jerry."

## What If This Is True?

Glazner's willingness to question accepted models became especially important in his work in Yosemite and the Sierra Nevada. By the 1990s, he and his students were testing the prevailing view that enormous bodies of granite had formed from large blobs of magma intruded over relatively short periods. The field evidence did not fit. Precise uranium-lead dating then produced another surprise: rocks thought to have formed within hundreds of thousands of years instead recorded histories spanning millions.

At first, Glazner and his colleagues assumed the data must be wrong.

They collected more samples. The results held.

Glazner remembers sitting alone near May Lake in Yosemite, writing in his notebook and asking himself a simple question: "What if this is true? What does it violate?"

The answer, he realized, was little beyond the prevailing model itself.

Scientific paradigms are powerful because they organize enormous amounts of information. Plate tectonics itself transformed geology by connecting observations that had once seemed unrelated. But useful ideas can also become difficult to abandon. Contradictory observations get labeled exceptions. New information is squeezed into an old framework. Eventually, Glazner argues, scientists have to be willing to ask whether the framework is the problem.

His current challenge to the traditional magma chamber is an extension of that thinking. In place of large, long-lived shallow reservoirs, Glazner argues that much magmatic activity is better understood through networks of dikes, fractures through which magma moves through the crust, and deeper zones where partially molten rock can persist. His lecture ranged from Yosemite plutons and Yellowstone to Iceland, Mercury, Venus, and Mars, but the underlying question remained the same: does the evidence really support the picture geologists have carried in their heads for generations?

## A Discipline Of Unanswered Questions

That question also shapes how Glazner thinks about teaching. His recent textbook, *Petrology and Plate Tectonics: An Earth Systems Approach*, coauthored with Matthew Kohn, closes with a section devoted to unsolved problems in the field.

"Students should not see geology as a catalog of settled facts," he said. "They should see a discipline full of unanswered questions. And themselves as people capable of answering them."

Since retiring from a 38-year faculty career at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Glazner has continued researching, writing, and teaching, while also sharing geology with wider audiences through field trips and Smithsonian Journeys. His return to UCLA gave him a chance to revisit the place where much of his scientific foundation was built.

He was struck by how much of the campus remained familiar, but also by how EPSS had evolved. In his conversation with the department, he praised its breadth across Earth, planetary, and space sciences and its willingness to continue pursuing fundamental questions across a wide range of disciplines.

That breadth matters to Glazner because the next scientific revolution rarely announces itself in advance. It may begin with a strange data point, a field observation that refuses to fit, or a student willing to ask why everyone assumes the textbook picture is correct.

For Glazner, that is where science gets interesting.
