Tiny differences between atoms can preserve evidence of processes that unfolded millions or even billions of years ago. The challenge is figuring out how to read them.

That challenge is at the center of Edwin Schauble's research. A professor of geochemistry and astrobiology in UCLA's Department of Earth, Planetary, and Space Sciences, Schauble is a theorist who studies isotopes, different forms of the same chemical element that vary slightly in mass. His work helps scientists understand why isotopes separate from one another in nature and what those differences can reveal about processes on Earth and other planets.

Isotope analysis has become one of geochemistry's most powerful tools. Consider carbon. Its two most abundant stable isotopes, carbon-12 and the slightly heavier carbon-13, behave almost identically, but not quite. During photosynthesis, plants preferentially incorporate carbon-12, leaving the resulting organic material with a different isotopic composition than the carbon dioxide from which it formed.

Similar sorting occurs during evaporation and condensation, chemical reactions, and the formation of minerals. Known as isotope fractionation, these small differences can leave signatures that scientists learn to read. Isotopes can help reconstruct past climates, trace the movement of water, and distinguish between different sources of carbon in the atmosphere.

But measuring an isotopic pattern is only part of the problem. Scientists also need to understand what physical or chemical process produced it. Schauble uses principles of chemistry, quantum mechanics, and thermodynamics to develop theoretical models that predict how isotopes should behave under different conditions.

Much of his career has involved extending that understanding beyond familiar elements such as carbon and oxygen to elements whose isotopic behavior has been far less thoroughly explored.

"The majority of the work I've done has been looking at new elements to see how isotope separation works in those elements," Schauble said.

That has meant studying isotope fractionation in elements including iron, chromium, uranium, and, more recently, rare earth elements. The questions are fundamental: If scientists find differences in the isotopic composition of iron in two materials, for example, what physical or chemical process might have produced them? What effects should researchers expect, and under what conditions?

Those questions have become increasingly relevant as laboratory instruments have made it possible to measure isotopic variations across much more of the periodic table. Schauble's role has often been to work alongside that expanding experimental frontier, developing the theory needed to interpret what researchers can now detect.

"It's like a map of an undiscovered territory," Schauble said.

## New Elements, New Clues

His recent work on the rare earth elements cerium and europium illustrates the challenge. Scientists can now measure naturally occurring isotopic variations in these elements, which are already useful for studying the formation and evolution of rocks on Earth and other planetary bodies. But what controls those variations is not yet fully understood. Schauble is using theoretical calculations to predict how the isotopes should separate, providing a framework against which new measurements can be compared.

Another branch of his research examines an even subtler isotopic phenomenon. Isotopes do not only separate between different substances; in some molecules, particular heavy isotopes can preferentially occur together, a phenomenon known as "clumping."

Working with UCLA professor Edward Young and other collaborators, Schauble has helped develop the theory behind clumped-isotope measurements of methane. Researchers can measure methane molecules containing both carbon-13 and deuterium, a heavy isotope of hydrogen, and use their abundance to learn about the chemical conditions under which the methane formed. Such measurements can help distinguish methane produced through different geological and biological processes.

The value of studying these sometimes obscure isotopic systems is that they may contain information scientists have not previously been able to access.

Earth is overwhelmingly composed of a relatively small number of elements, making it natural that scientists historically concentrated on isotope systems such as carbon and oxygen. But an element does not have to be abundant to be informative. In the right circumstances, a trace element can record a process occurring around it with remarkable sensitivity.

As scientists become capable of measuring ever smaller variations in ever more elements, Schauble's theoretical work helps determine what those variations are telling us, about the reactions that produced them, the conditions under which they formed, and the history they preserve.
