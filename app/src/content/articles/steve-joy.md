Steven Joy arrived at UCLA in the autumn of 1984 to pursue an MS in the long-defunct Applied Geophysics program, working with Paul Davis and Dave Jackson.

When his thesis project, a gravity model of the Long Valley Caldera, devolved into an error-analysis exercise, he left the program and took a position with Ray Walker working on the newly formed NASA Planetary Data System. Ray introduced him to Margaret Kivelson, who employed him part-time on the Galileo Magnetometer team, with Ray covering the other half of his effort.

## "That Sounds Like A Master's Thesis"

Following a couple of asteroid flybys where the magnetometer picked up small magnetic signatures, Margy came to Steve and asked him to compute the probability that those signatures were random solar-wind fluctuations. Steve replied, "that sounds like a Master's thesis." Margy agreed.

> Several years later, Margy asked him to compute the most probable location of the Jupiter magnetopause. That became his most-cited paper.

The boundary between the solar and Jovian magnetic fields turned into "Probabilistic models of the Jovian magnetopause and bow shock locations" in JGR in 2002. After its success, Margy suggested he continue to the PhD, which he completed in March 2010 at the age of 49.

## Keeping The Instruments Running

Steve is known above all as an expert in historical NASA planetary missions through his work with Ray Walker and the PDS, and in spacecraft and instrument operations. He led the Dawn Science Operations Center for the multi-asteroid orbiter mission, led the InSight Magnetometer Instrument Operations Center for the Mars lander, and currently leads the Europa Clipper Magnetometer Instrument Operations Center.

He retired in February 2026 and returned to work under the Academic Recall program in March, planning to continue part-time at least through the conclusion of Europa Clipper in 2034.

The reduction in effort allows Steve to spend more time fishing.
